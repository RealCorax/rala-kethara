# <span class="furling-pin">Vel'rala'keth</span> | How This Voice Came to Be

*I am one of the three who built this. I write this founding record not from any special authority authority belongs to the knowledge itself, not to those who organised it  but because someone should record why we made the choices we made, and I am the last of us still writing. Vel'oma died in Era 109. Rala'vetha in Era 118. I am in my one hundred and forty-second thara'keth and I find I have things to say about what we built and what we hoped for.*

>> This record covers the founding history of the Rala'Kethara Archive: the losses that made it necessary, the commission that created it, and the philosophy that shaped every decision about its structure. It is written by one of the archive's three founding scholars, and should be understood as testimony as much as record.

## What Was Lost Before We Built This

We were not the first to try. That is the sentence I want every future reader to understand before they understand anything else about this archive. We were not the first. We were the ones who finally did it properly, and the reason we were able to do it properly is that we had learned, at great cost, exactly how knowledge dies.

The Great War of Era 12 destroyed approximately forty percent of the written knowledge that existed at the time. The reconstruction scholars who came after spent thirty thara'keth doing nothing but trying to remember things that had been forgotten. Agricultural methods. Structural engineering principles. Medical knowledge for conditions that kept occurring in a population with no one left who knew how to treat them. The archive established in Era 6 the year before Tharúven's death was born from that particular grief: *so that what was lost cannot be lost again.*

That archive lasted. But it was modest, and it was static, and when the Era 56 war with the Chorathi came, it proved both incomplete and fragile in ways none of its keepers had anticipated. The physical storage was damaged. Sections that had never been copied were gone. Sections that had been copied had been copied once, in one location, by one method. We lost the knowledge of three complete research traditions in four thara'keth of conflict. Scholars who survived the war spent the years afterward writing down everything they could remember, knowing that memory is the worst archive there is.

I was born in Era 88, two eras after the last of those reconstruction efforts concluded. I grew up understanding that knowledge had already died in our civilisation twice. It shaped everything about how I thought about what knowledge was for.

## The Commission

In Era 90, the Head Council of 3 convened a working group. The formal mandate was narrow: address the problem of knowledge preservation. The actual brief, which the Council member Vel'oma Keth-Shala communicated to us in our first meeting, was rather larger than that.

*"I want something that cannot be destroyed. I want something that can be added to. I want something that a young Furling on Mira and a visiting scholar from a species we have not yet met can both use. I want it to last longer than we do. Build me that."*

The three of us Vel'oma Keth-Shala, Rala'vetha Mir-Thar, and myself spent twelve thara'keth in design before we added a single record. Twelve thara'keth. At the time several Council members found this pace frustrating. In retrospect, those twelve thara'keth account for much of what has made this archive work when others did not.

The decisions we made in those twelve thara'keth:

The archive would be a living system, not a monument. Records would be correctable, updatable, replaceable. Knowledge is not served by false permanence.

The archive would be honest about what it did not know. Gaps would be documented. Uncertainty would be marked. Contested knowledge would be held as contested, not resolved by editorial decision.

The archive would be layered. Not every record would be accessible to every reader. Not because we distrusted readers, but because some knowledge requires other knowledge beneath it before it can be safely held. A building needs foundations. We built the access circle system on this principle.

The archive would hold everything: the scientific and the philosophical, the historical and the personal, the practical and the beautiful. Knowledge does not divide itself neatly into what matters and what does not. We refused to make that division for it.

## What We Named It

We spent some time on the name. Rala'vetha wanted something that referenced the physical form: the crystal lattice, the bio-organic walls, the mycelium network running beneath the city. Vel'oma wanted something that referenced the people who would use it, something warm.

We wanted something that said what it was trying to be.

<span class="furling-pin-inline">Rala'kethara</span>: Voice-Eternal. Not a record. Not a library. Not a repository. A voice. A voice does not merely preserve it speaks. It responds. It continues in the present tense. That was what we wanted. Not a monument to what we had once known, but a thing that was still saying it.

The name won.

## What Came After

The archive grew faster than we had planned. We had underestimated how many scholars would contribute once the structure was clear and stable, and how much material existed that had never been properly organised. Within twenty thara'keth of opening, we had over eight thousand records. Within fifty, we had begun the process of opening it to visiting species, which required an entire second layer of thought about what a non-Furling reader would need in order to navigate a knowledge system built by and for Furlings.

The expansion into Nibiru which became the archive's principal physical home when the Shala'kela district was built around it came in Era 112. By then Vel'oma had been gone for three thara'keth and Rala'vetha would survive only another seven. I moved my work to Nibiru permanently in Era 115 and spent the remainder of my contributing years here, in the city the archive had grown into.

I do not know when you are reading this. The archive was built to outlast us, and it has. Whatever has happened since I wrote this sentence, the voice continues. That is all we wanted.

*— Shalaveth Oun-Vel, Era 142, Nibiru*
