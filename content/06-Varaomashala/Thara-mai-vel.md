# <span class="furling-pin">Thara'mai'vel</span> | The Names We Gave to Light and Ground

> *Long before we understood what a planet was, we named the lights in the sky. We gave them personalities, histories, relationships with each other. When science eventually told us what they actually were, we kept the stories — not because we had forgotten that they were stories, but because the stories were still doing their work.*
> — Vel Thara-Omaven, Era 99

## Vara — The Patient One

The sun was always the sun. Before it was Vara — before it had a name or a classification — it was simply the light that made life possible, the warmth that told seeds to open, the thing that was there every morning and could be trusted.

In the oldest layers of Furling mythology, Vara is not a being but a quality: the constancy of warmth. Later traditions made it a figure — ancient, calm, endlessly attentive, the parent that never tired and never failed. In some versions Vara chose to be a sun deliberately, selecting a form of existence that could sustain life for billions of thara'keth without needing anything in return. In others Vara simply *is* that way, as naturally as water flows downward, and the constancy is not a sacrifice but a nature.

The astronomical record tells us Vara is a K4V main sequence star approximately four tael'kora old, with six to nine tael'kora remaining. The myth tells us Vara is patient. Both are true.

## Vareth — The Threshold Figure

The closest world to Vara, tidally locked: one side in permanent blazing light, the other in permanent cold darkness. Its surface gleams with exposed crystal and iron.

In ancient Furling myth, Vareth was a being who stood at the threshold of Vara itself, seeking to understand the sun's nature by approaching it as closely as possible. They were not destroyed — that would be too simple a story. They were transformed: their body became something between flesh and metal, hot on one side, cold on the other, unable to leave the boundary between light and dark because that boundary had become their body.

Furling moral philosophy used Vareth as a figure for a specific kind of obsession: the pursuit of understanding that loses sight of what the understanding is for.

## Thrael — The Fire That Gave Itself Away

The second world: hot, volcanic, still slowly destroying itself from the inside.

The myth: Thrael was a being of immense creative energy who poured so much of themselves into shaping the world that they burned hollow from the inside. The Furling phrase that comes from this: *"To give all your fire is to become a husk of heat."* Used as a caution against a specific kind of burnout — not exhaustion from too much work, but the particular hollowness of someone who has invested so completely in a project that they have nothing left for themselves or anyone close to them.

## Mira — Home

No elaborate myth. The most simply held. Mira is home. In the oldest oral traditions, Mira is never a character — it is the ground beneath every character's feet, the thing that holds the story up.

The sacredness of Mira in Furling spiritual tradition was not formalised into doctrine because it did not need to be. You do not need doctrine to love the ground under your feet. It is simply there, and it holds you, and eventually you understand that this is a remarkable thing for anything to do.

The loss of Mira — taken by the Keth'alis during the war — is not recorded with the appropriate weight in any single document. The archive holds thousands of records about Mira. They end. Not with a record of the loss. They simply end, mid-life, as if the people writing them stepped away from their desks. Which is what happened.

## The Velakris Belt — Fragments of an Old Dream

On clear nights on Mira, the asteroid belt was visible as a faint glittering arc when Vara's light caught the iron-crystal debris near the horizon. Ancient Furlings called it *velakris* before they understood what it was. The name stuck.

Children were told: the sky-crystals are fragments of an old dream Mira had, before she learned to hold herself together. This is not a disturbing story. It is a gentle one. Mira dreamed something too large for herself, and it scattered outward, and it catches the light now in the evening sky.

## Thalavar — The Silent Protector

The great gas giant of the outer system. Its gravitational mass shields the inner system from the majority of deep-space impacts that would otherwise reach Mira.

The myth: the great protector, standing at the outer threshold so that Mira and her siblings could live in peace. Patient, vast, largely invisible — doing essential work that almost no one thinks about. Thalavar became a common figure in Furling moral philosophy: the one who protects without recognition, without glory, simply because it is their nature. The highest compliment in certain Furling traditions is to call someone *thalavar'vel* — a Thalavar-life.
