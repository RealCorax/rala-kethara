# <span class="furling-pin">Vetha'mai'shala'vel</span> | The Quiet Practice of Turning Inward

> *I will not call what I am describing meditation, because that word carries too many associations from too many traditions and most of them are inaccurate. What I am describing is simpler: the practice of noticing. Not thinking about what you notice. Not analysing it or evaluating it or adding anything to it. Noticing it. Most people can do this for about thirty thaeli before the mind interrupts. The practice is returning, every time it does, to the noticing.*
> — Omavel Reva-Thara, Era 125

## What the Practice Is

Furling contemplative life is not primarily about withdrawal from the world. It is about return to it — but more accurately. The practice of turning inward is not an escape from engagement. It is the preparation for better engagement: attending to your own state, your own patterns of thought and response, so that when you turn outward again you are not projecting those patterns onto what you are encountering.

The foundational practice has no Furling name as specific as its English approximation "contemplative practice" — it is simply described as <span class="furling-pin-inline">vel'shola'mai</span>, "holding the blessed still." The name describes the quality being cultivated: a stillness that is held, not imposed; that contains rather than empties.

It is done in any position that allows the body to be settled without falling asleep. Furlings have no tradition of specific postures or specific breath techniques, because these were understood as distractions from the actual practice: the quality of attention, not the body's configuration.

---

## Three Forms

**Vel'nai — Still Water**

The most common practice. The practitioner settles into stillness and attends to whatever is present: sounds, sensations, the movements of thought. They do not try to stop thought. They simply notice when attention has been carried away by a thought, and return to the direct experience of what is actually happening in the body and the space.

The return is the practice. Not the stillness — the stillness is the ideal. The practice is the returning.

This is typically done in periods of fifty to a hundred thaeli — approximately eight to seventeen minutes.

**Vel'thara — Moving Through**

A walking practice. The attention is placed on the direct experience of movement: the weight shift of each step, the sensations of air on the skin, the sounds and sights of the environment as they actually are rather than as remembered or anticipated. When attention wanders, it is returned to the immediate experience of moving.

In Nibiru, this is done in the quieter walking paths through the garden spaces. The farm district, overgrown now but still living, provides the closest equivalent to the grove experience for visitors who want it.

**Vel'rala'shola — Speaking What Is**

A partner practice. Two people sit together; one speaks, in simple present-tense sentences, what they are experiencing right now — not analysing it, not narrating it, simply describing it as directly as possible. The other listens without responding. After a set period, they switch.

The practice develops two skills simultaneously: the ability to speak accurately about immediate experience, and the ability to listen to another person's experience without immediately translating it into your own. Both are harder than they sound. Both are more useful than most other things you can practise.

## On Consistency

Every practitioner of contemplative life discovers the same thing: the practice is most useful precisely when it seems hardest to do. When the day is difficult, when the mind is busy, when everything demands attention — this is exactly when the return to stillness is most valuable. Not because it solves the difficulty. Because it establishes a brief period of direct experience uncomplicated by the overlay of narrative and reaction, from which the difficulty can be approached more clearly.

Furling practitioners traditionally did not speak about their practice in terms of achievement. There is nothing to achieve. The practice simply is what it is, done regularly, and the effects accumulate without being tracked. A Furling who had practised vel'nai every morning for forty thara'keth would not say they were advanced at it. They would say they had done it forty thara'keth worth of times.

This is the correct way to understand it.
