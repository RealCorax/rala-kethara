# <span class="furling-pin">Vel Omara-Shal</span> | Educator and Children's Literature Scholar

> [!person-portrait]
> Era 88–144 · Mira, Omavel Settlement · Children's Education, Early Literature, Oral Tradition

## Life and Work

Vel Omara-Shal spent most of their working life teaching children aged three to ten thara'keth in a mid-sized community in Mira's eastern settlements. They were not, for most of that time, an archivist or a scholar. They were a practising teacher who found, in the course of teaching, that the materials available for use with very young learners were inadequately documented and increasingly difficult to locate as communities spread further from Mira's central archive systems.

The practical problem — teachers not being able to find the works they wanted to use — produced the scholarly one: why were these works hard to find, and what had been lost? Vel began documenting the children's works held in oral tradition in their region, working with elder community members to transcribe pieces that had never been formally written down. This project, begun as a personal effort in Era 105, grew into a collaboration with the regional archive that eventually produced the most comprehensive survey of Furling children's literature assembled since Era 67.

The Nibiru connection came in Era 122, when Vel was invited to contribute to the archive's visitor-facing materials for the Vara'oma'shala branch. Their assessment of the existing materials was characteristically direct: the branch held extraordinary content and explained it in ways that made it feel distant and curated rather than immediate and real. The children's works section they compiled for the visitor level was an attempt to correct this. They chose works not because they were the most historically significant but because they still worked — because someone sitting in front of them now would still feel what they were made to make a person feel.

Vel returned to Mira in Era 130 to continue their regional documentation work. They were on Mira when the war reached it. Their fate is not recorded.

## A Note on Their Approach

Vel Omara-Shal believed that the correct test for whether a children's work was any good was simple: does it still do what it was made to do? Not "is it historically significant," not "does it transmit the correct values" — does it reach the person it was made for? They applied this test to every work they chose to feature, including the ones they had been using in their own classroom for thirty thara'keth. The works in the visitor-level collection passed.
