# 02.01.03 The Cycles That Shape a Furling Life | Biological Development and Life Stages

*Compiled by Vel'thara Kelomis, Era 44. Expanded by Shala'vetha Oriskan, Era 97.*

Furling life is traditionally divided into five stages. The boundaries between them are biological, social, and ceremonial. Each stage carries distinct responsibilities and freedoms within the greater-family structure.

## Kinara -- Child (0 to 10 thara'keth)

The word kinara means child and carries within it the root kina -- an archaic word meaning one who is held. Children accompany their parents to their work from the earliest age at which they are capable of doing so safely. They observe, assist where possible, and absorb. Children also attend local mixed-age group training. Older children have explicit responsibility to care for younger ones, a practice that teaches the value of contribution before formal adulthood begins.

## Grown Child (11 to 17 thara'keth)

Advanced teaching from dedicated educators replaces the parent-focused observation of early childhood. Community duties begin formally during this period: recycling, care for aged members, household responsibilities shared across the greater-family.

## Adult (18 thara'keth onward)

At 18 thara'keth a Furling receives the coming-of-age status marker and the right to choose a dedicated field of study and work. The Furlings do not separate these two things. The ceremony is communal -- all those reaching adulthood in nearby areas celebrate together on a chosen day. It is not an individual milestone. It belongs to the cohort.

All Furlings regardless of field are required to contribute 100 thaeli per thara'keth (approximately 20 Earth hours per year) to greater-family support.

## Grown Adult (ongoing)

The longest and most productive stage. With natural lifespans of 200 thara'keth and scientifically extended lifespans of 400 to 500, a Furling grown adult has decades or centuries in which to develop their chosen contribution. The depth of knowledge achievable in 100 thara'keth of dedicated study in a single field is difficult to overstate.

## Senior (advanced age)

Respected and cared for by the community, with no expectation of formal contribution but often continuing voluntary engagement with their field into very late life. With extended lifespans it is possible for four and five generations of a greater-family to overlap simultaneously.
