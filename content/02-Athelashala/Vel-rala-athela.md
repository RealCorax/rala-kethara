# <span class="furling-pin">Vel'rala'athela</span> | Origins of the Furling Language

> *"The oldest words we have are not in any language we can read fluently. They are scratched into cave walls in a system that took three generations of scholars to partially decode. And yet when I read them, I know what they are reaching for. The same things we are always reaching for."*

>> This record covers the prehistoric origins of Furling speech, the surviving fragments of Proto-Furling, and the long process by which a spoken tradition became a written one. For the grammar of the living language, see the companion record on Modern High Furling.

## Before Writing

The earliest evidence of Furling language comes not from texts but from cave art at sites across the Miravel continent — the oldest of which, Vel'shael-Nai, predates Era 0 by at least sixty thara'keth. Among the images are repeated marks that linguists now believe to be the earliest surviving notation: not phonetic writing, but a system of marks indicating category, direction, and quantity.

These marks were not yet language in the formal sense. They were the beginning of the impulse: to make something visible that was otherwise only spoken, so it could outlast the moment of its saying.

Oral tradition from the pre-writing period was extensive and structured. The practice of formalised recitation — repeating accounts in fixed patterns so they could be transmitted accurately — is evident in the earliest transcribed traditions. We were keeping records long before we had a way to write them down. We held them in the patterns of sound itself.

## Proto-Furling

The first writing system that linguists can identify as a genuine ancestor of the modern language emerges from the Reconstruction Period following the Great War. This is not coincidence. The Great War destroyed so much of what had been held in living memory that the survivors understood, immediately, that oral tradition alone could not be trusted to carry what remained. Writing was not invented after the Great War. It was made urgent.

The Proto-Furling fragments that survive from this period — primarily on crystal-slate tablets, some on shaped stone — show a system that is recognisably related to the modern language but distinct in its structure. The core roots are present: *vetha, skela, shala, oma, mira, vara*. The compound formation logic is present. The flow particle exists in rudimentary form. What is absent is the full evidential system, the three registers, and much of the phonological refinement that would come over the following eras.

[[Tharúven Kora'thesh]], the unifier whose work gave the post-war Furlings the foundations of their rebuilt civilisation, is credited in most historical accounts with commissioning the first formal writing project: the transcription of the oral traditions that remained, in a systematic notation, before the people who carried them were also gone. The result was the first written archive. Small, incomplete, damaged. But present.

## The Long Refinement

From Era 0 onward, the language developed through a process that is unusually well documented compared to its pre-war origins, because the post-war Furlings were determined to document everything they could.

The central tension in this development was between the natural drift of a spoken language and the desire for a formal written standard. Spoken language changes; communities separated by distance develop different habits; words shift in meaning over generations. The scholars who maintained the written record had to decide, repeatedly, whether to follow the spoken language or to hold a standard against it.

The answer the archive settled on was neither pure prescription nor pure description: the written archive maintained a formal register that was deliberately stable, while acknowledging in its records that spoken language varied and changed. The result was the three-register system that characterises Modern High Furling. Archive register preserves; everyday register lives; educated register bridges between them.

## What the Oldest Words Tell Us

When [[Miravel Oun-Nai]] and the language division reviewed the earliest fragments in Era 130 as part of the pre-archive consolidation, one observation stood out in the published findings:

The very oldest surviving roots are not for things. They are for relationships. *Meva* (give). *Renar* (help). *Oma* (peace). *Skela* (belonging). The words for objects — stone, water, star, ground — came later in the surviving record, or were reconstructed from compounds.

Whether this reflects the actual order in which Furling speakers first named things, or simply which words were considered important enough to preserve, is an open question. But we note it. The language, in its oldest surviving form, was already organised around what flows between beings rather than what any being possesses alone.
