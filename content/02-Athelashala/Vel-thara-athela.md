# <span class="furling-pin">Vel'thara'athela</span> | Timekeeping and the Furling Calendar

> *"We count time in cycles because that is what time is. The light returns. The growing season returns. The orbital path closes and opens again. A line from past to future is a useful abstraction. A cycle is a description of what actually happens."*

>> This record covers the Furling system of timekeeping — the units of time, how they are observed and marked, and how the calendar functions across different worlds with different orbital periods. For the philosophical treatment of time and memory, see the record at Student access.

## The Basic Units

All Furling time measurement is ultimately anchored to physical constants of [[Mira]] and [[Vara]].

The <span class="furling-pin-inline">tael</span> is the smallest standard unit — derived from the resonance frequency of kris'thral crystal under standard conditions. It is a very short unit, the base from which all others are built.

The <span class="furling-pin-inline">thaeli</span> is the practical working unit for short tasks — approximately eleven tael. When a Furling says something will take a thaeli, they mean a brief, defined period.

The <span class="furling-pin-inline">mira'thara</span> is one complete rotation of Mira on its own axis — the standard short-cycle unit. What other species might call a day. Named for the world turning against its star.

The <span class="furling-pin-inline">thara'keth</span> is one complete orbit of Mira around Vara — the standard long-cycle unit. What other species might call a year. All ages, historical dates, and durations of significance are measured in thara'keth.

## Across Multiple Worlds

One of the practical challenges of a multi-world civilisation is that different worlds orbit their stars at different speeds and rotate at different rates. A settlement on a world with a shorter orbital period experiences more thara'keth by local reckoning than Mira does in the same period.

The resolution the archive uses is consistent: all dating and duration references use Mira's thara'keth as the standard. Local communities keep their own cycles and know the conversion. Historical records, archive entries, and inter-world communication all use the Mira standard. A scholar on a world with a different orbital period knows exactly how their local cycles translate.

This is not a political decision — it is simply the convenience of having a shared reference. The choice of Mira is historical: it was the first world, the homeworld, and its measurements were the first measurements. No other argument was needed.

## The Shape of a Day

A mira'thara is divided into periods of light and dark by the rotation of the world. In artificial environments like enclosed city installations, this cycle is maintained by systems that regulate the intensity and colour of bioluminescent lighting to follow a natural progression — the warming light of the cycle's opening, the full illumination of its middle period, the cooling into darkness and rest.

Furlings orient their daily activity around this cycle. Working periods tend to follow the light. Rest is associated with darkness. The rhythm is not rigid — Furlings whose work or preference differs from the standard cycle are accommodated — but the architecture and shared life of a community follows it as a default.

## Marking Time in History

Historical dating in the archive uses the era system: Era 0 being the Reconstruction Period following the Great War, with negative era numbers for the prehistoric period and positive numbers forward from there. Most significant events in Furling history are located by their era, with more precise thara'keth notation where the records support it.

The full archive timeline — when each section was compiled, which scholars contributed at which period, how knowledge accumulated across the eras — is in Division 08. What is noted here is the underlying principle: we mark time because things happened in it, and when things happened matters to understanding what they were.
