# <span class="furling-pin">Ketha'oma'athela</span> | Justice — Restoration Rather Than Punishment

> *"We have a word for the harm that punishment adds to harm. We do not have a word for a justice that does not eventually make something whole. That absence is not an accident."*

>> This record covers the Furling philosophy and practice of justice. For the philosophical foundations that underpin it, see the record on the Kora'thesh Veshúnar.

## What Justice Is For

The fifth principle of the Kora'thesh Veshúnar states: *"What is broken between living things must be made whole."* This is the foundation of everything we call justice.

Justice in Furling understanding is not primarily concerned with assigning blame or delivering suffering proportional to suffering caused. It is concerned with the state of the community after harm has occurred. Has the harm been honestly named? Has the person harmed been genuinely heard? Has the person who caused harm understood what they did and why it was wrong? Has something been done to make the situation better? These are the questions our justice asks.

This is not softness. It is a harder standard than punishment, because it requires real change rather than the performance of suffering.

## How It Works in Practice

When harm occurs in a Furling community, the immediate response is to bring those involved into conversation facilitated by a trained mediator — a role called <span class="furling-pin-inline">oma'renar</span>, peace-helper. The mediator's function is not to judge but to ensure that all voices are fully heard and that the conversation reaches a genuine resolution rather than a formal one.

The process cannot be rushed. This is built in as a feature rather than a flaw. A resolution reached before genuine understanding has been achieved is not a resolution — it is a postponement. The archive holds records of communities that attempted to accelerate their justice processes and found that the underlying harm simply re-emerged later, in larger form.

The outcome of a justice process is usually a combination of: acknowledged responsibility by the person who caused harm, genuine understanding of that harm's effect, practical measures to address what was damaged, and where possible, restored relationship. The archive does not pretend that restored relationship is always achievable. Sometimes harm is too severe. But it is always the direction we move toward.

## Serious Harm

For the most serious harms — violence, deliberate exploitation, betrayal of deep trust — the process is more formal and involves the Council directly. Separation of the person who caused harm from the community may be necessary for the safety of others.

Even here, the question is not how much the person should suffer but what the community requires to be safe and whole. A person who is a danger to others is separated. Separation is not abandonment — the expectation of genuine change, and the possibility of return, is preserved unless the harm was so severe that return cannot safely be offered.

There are harms for which Furling justice has no complete answer. The archive records these honestly. The most difficult cases — those involving harm to children, deliberate and premeditated exploitation of trust, or actions that caused irreversible damage to other beings — are acknowledged as situations in which restoration in the full sense is not achievable. What justice can do in those cases is ensure the harm is honestly named, the community is protected, and what can be repaired is repaired.

## What We Do Not Do

We do not inflict suffering as a response to suffering on the grounds that proportionality constitutes justice. The archive contains philosophical examination of this position — why it is held, what arguments have been made for it across our history, and why we have consistently rejected it. The examination is at Student access.

We do not treat the appearance of justice as equivalent to justice itself. A process that has the right form but produces no genuine change or restoration is not justice. It is administration.
