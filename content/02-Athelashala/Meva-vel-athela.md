# <span class="furling-pin">Meva'vel'athela</span> | Food, Growing and Communal Eating

> *"The starter culture in my kitchen has been alive for longer than most of the settlements we have established on colony worlds. I feed it. It feeds us. This seems like the right kind of relationship to be in."*

>> This record covers Furling diet, agricultural philosophy, the abandoned food synthesis programme, and the central place of communal eating in Furling daily life. Three example recipes from the archive are included at the end.

## What We Eat

We are herbivores. This is not primarily a philosophical position, though it aligns with our values — it is biological. Animal-based food does not agree with Furling digestion and holds no appeal to our senses. We grow everything we eat.

Furling agriculture focuses on: root vegetables and tubers, dense and storable, foundational to most meals; leafy plants and herbs, used fresh and in large variety; fruits and seed-bearing plants; grain-equivalents, ground and used in flatbreads, porridge-style dishes, and fermented preparations; fungi, extensively cultivated and a major source of the compounds Furling biology needs for sustained energy; legume-equivalents; and flowering plants, some edible, some medicinal, some grown purely for beauty alongside food.

We do not separate ornamental and food growing. A garden that is only practical is considered incomplete.

## The Abandoned Path

In approximately Era 60, a significant scientific effort was made to develop food replication technology — the ability to produce nutritionally complete food without growing. It succeeded technically. What was produced was edible. But long-term studies showed measurable nutritional gaps that growing did not produce, and the subjective experience of eating synthesised food was described by participants as *"correct but empty."*

The programme was formally ended in approximately Era 72. The archive retains the research as a historical record. The conclusion is stated plainly in the closing report: *"Growing is not an inefficiency to be engineered away. It is part of what food is."*

## How We Eat

Eating alone is rare and slightly uncomfortable — not shameful, but notable. A Furling eating alone is typically unwell, in a period of private difficulty, or working through something they need quiet for. Others will generally check in, gently, without making it into an event.

Communal eating is simply how we eat. The meal is social. Conversation, sharing of what was learned that day, discussing a problem together — these are what eating is for, as much as the food. A silent meal is almost unknown except in grief.

## Three Recipes

**Vel'mira Thesh — The Ground Bread**
Grind dried grain-root until it forms a coarse flour. Mix with water until a firm dough forms — it should hold its shape when pressed. Add a small amount of cultured starter kept from the previous batch. Press flat — the thickness of two fingers. Cook on a warm stone surface until the underside forms a crust, then turn once.

Dense, slightly bitter, deeply filling. Eaten with fresh herbs pressed into the surface while still warm, or with preserved root paste, or simply alone. Every kitchen has its starter. Every family's starter tastes different. I have eaten this bread on seven worlds and it was never quite the same twice.

**Nai'vetha Shal — The River Herb Preparation**
Gather fresh water-herbs — the broad-leafed variety that grows in slow-moving water. Crush lightly to release the oils. Steep in warm, not boiling, water for approximately one thaeli. The resulting liquid is pale green and tastes of clean water with something sharper underneath.

Drink warm. Used for mild digestive discomfort, as a gentle restorative after physical exertion, and in social contexts as a contemplative drink — something to hold and sip while thinking or talking.

**Meva'shala Vel — The Sharing Bowl**
Slow-cook root vegetables (three or four varieties, cut large) in water with dried fungi and a handful of spice-leaves until the roots are soft throughout. Add fresh leafy greens in the final moments — they should wilt slightly but remain bright.

This is not a dish with a fixed recipe. It is an approach. What goes into it depends on what is available, what is in season, who is cooking, and who is eating. Make something warm. Make enough. Put it in the middle. Let everyone reach for what they need.
