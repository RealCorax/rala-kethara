# <span class="furling-pin">Shala'vetha'athela</span> | Furling Ethics — The Living Practice

> *"Ethics is not a set of rules we consult when we are uncertain. It is a habit of attention we cultivate when we are certain, so that when uncertainty arrives we are already turned toward it in the right way."*

>> This record covers the practical application of Furling ethics — how the principles of the Kora'thesh Veshúnar are lived in daily decisions, and what the Furling tradition says about the hardest cases. For the philosophical foundations, see the companion records at Student access.

## Intent Over Action

The foundational Furling ethical position is that intent matters more than action in assessing what has occurred. This is not a position that excuses harmful outcomes. It is a position that locates responsibility accurately.

A Furling who causes harm while genuinely trying to help is in a different moral situation from one who causes the same harm deliberately. Both situations require response. But they require different responses. The first requires honest examination of what went wrong and how to do better. The second requires the full engagement of the justice process.

This distinction is not always comfortable to maintain. The most difficult cases in Furling ethical history involve situations where good intent produced catastrophic harm. The archive holds those cases honestly. Intent matters — and intent is not sufficient. Both things are true.

## The Honest Middle Ground

Furling ethical language has a formal position for genuine uncertainty: <span class="furling-pin-inline">thala'shola</span> — I hold this carefully, I am uncertain. It is considered more honest, and more courageous, to say this than to perform a confidence one does not have.

The evidential particle <span class="furling-pin-inline">·thala</span> in the language serves the same function in written records. A claim marked ·thala is not a weak claim. It is an honest one. An archive that does not mark its uncertainties is an archive that cannot be trusted, because it has not told you where to stop relying on it.

This position has direct ethical consequence. A Furling who acts with certainty they do not possess — who makes a consequential decision as if the outcome were known when it is not — bears greater responsibility for harm that results than one who acted with genuine acknowledgment of what was unknown. Pretending to certainty is, in Furling ethics, a form of dishonesty with consequences.

## The Weight of Responsibility

The second principle of the Kora'thesh Veshúnar holds that power — in any form — belongs to everyone and must never be withheld from those who need it. The corollary is that those who hold power in any form — knowledge, physical strength, governance authority, medical skill — bear a greater responsibility for how they use it.

A Furling healer who knows something useful and does not share it is not neutral. A Council member who makes a decision affecting thousands and does not genuinely engage with its consequences is not neutral. In Furling ethics, the position of greater capability always comes with the position of greater obligation.

This applies to the archive itself. Everything held here at higher access levels is held because the archivist believed that releasing it without the right foundation could cause harm. That belief is itself a form of power, and it is held as a responsibility rather than a privilege.

## What Furling Ethics Does Not Do

It does not provide clean answers to hard questions. The archive is full of records of situations in which the principles conflicted with each other — where tending life required withholding knowledge, where sharing knowledge required risking harm, where restoration was not possible. These records are preserved because the honest engagement with difficulty is itself the practice of ethics.

It does not excuse past harm because of good intentions. The history of our contact with the Athel'kin is the most significant example of this in our record. The intent was genuine. The harm was real. Both things are in the archive, together, because both things are true.
