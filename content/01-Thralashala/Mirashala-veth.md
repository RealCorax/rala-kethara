# <span class="furling-pin">Mirashala'veth</span> | Planetary Science

> *The question people ask most often about planetary science is: how do you find a habitable world? I understand why — if you are in a ship looking for somewhere to land, or planning a mission across the galaxy, habitability is the practical question. But the science that allows you to answer it was built by people who were not looking for anywhere. They were simply trying to understand what worlds are, what kinds of worlds exist, and how they come to be what they are. The practical use followed from the understanding, as it usually does.*
> — Miravel Thara-Vel, Era 96

## What Planetary Science Studies

Planetary science is the study of planets, moons, and smaller bodies — their formation, their physical properties, their geological and atmospheric evolution, and, where relevant, their capacity to support life.

It draws on multiple sciences: the physics of gravitational formation and orbital mechanics, the chemistry of rock and atmospheric composition, the geology of interior processes, and the biology of habitability. A planetary record — like those in the Vara'skela system data tables — represents the synthesis of all these approaches applied to a specific world.

## How Planets Form

Stars and planets form from the same cloud of gas and dust. As the cloud collapses under its own gravity to form a star, the remaining material flattens into a disk around the forming star. Within the disk, dust grains collide and stick together; these aggregates grow through further collisions into bodies that are large enough for gravity to assist the process; eventually some grow large enough to clear their orbital zones and become planets.

The composition of what forms in each zone of the disk depends on temperature. Close to the star, where temperatures are high, only high-melting-point materials survive in solid form — metals and silicates, the stuff of rocky planets. Further out, where temperatures are lower, ices of water, carbon dioxide, methane, and other volatile compounds can survive, and planets that form there incorporate these materials in much larger quantities.

This is why the Vara'skela system has the structure it does: rocky planets close in (Vareth, Thrael, Mira), an asteroid belt where a planet failed to form, and ice-rich bodies and gas giants in the outer system (Naiveth, Thalavar, Velathara). The same process has produced similar general structures in most star systems in the survey.

## Habitability

A habitable world is one that can sustain liquid water at its surface — the minimal condition for the kind of chemistry that life, as documented across the archive, runs on. This requires a planet to be at the right distance from its star (in the habitable zone, where temperatures permit liquid water), to have sufficient atmospheric pressure to prevent that water from boiling off, and to have geological activity sufficient to recycle the atmospheric gases that regulate temperature.

Mira satisfies all of these conditions. Its position at 1.00 skela'var from Vara places it within the habitable zone for a K4V star. Its atmosphere maintains the pressure and composition needed for liquid surface water. Its active tectonics continuously recycle carbon through the crust and atmosphere, maintaining the temperature regulation that keeps the climate stable across long periods.

The survey has found that habitable worlds are not common in absolute terms — they are a small fraction of all planetary bodies. But the galaxy contains so many planetary systems that the absolute number of potentially habitable worlds is very large. Exactly how many of those worlds are actually inhabited is a question the archive cannot fully answer, because survey observation can identify habitability but not life at most distances.

## Reading a Planetary Record

The data tables in the Vara'skela system data document are the format used for planetary records throughout the archive. Each attribute is defined and measured in Furling standard units. Reading them requires knowing what the units mean (see [[Furling-Units-of-Measurement|Furling Units of Measurement]]) and what the attributes indicate.

A few key values and what they tell you:

**Orbital distance in skela'var** — where the world sits relative to Mira's position. A value less than 1.0 means closer to Vara than Mira; greater than 1.0 means further out.

**Surface gravity in mira'thrala** — how the world's gravity compares to Mira's. Values below 0.7 or above 1.5 mira'thrala are likely to make extended habitation by Furling-type species uncomfortable without adaptation.

**Atmosphere pressure in vela'thrala** — the density of the atmosphere. 0 means no atmosphere; values below about 0.3 are too thin for unassisted breathing; values well above 1.0 may create pressure problems depending on atmospheric composition.

**Temperature range in lath** — Mira's comfortable Furling ambient is approximately 110–120 lath. Worlds whose range falls mostly outside this band will require protective equipment or habitat construction for comfortable occupation.

The individual body records in this section provide full data for each body in the Vara'skela system. Those records are the starting point for understanding what each world is like in physical terms.
