## What Kind of Star Vara Is

Vara is a K-type main sequence star — what Furling stellar science classifies as a warm, stable, long-lived orange dwarf. It sits between the more common red dwarfs and the brighter, shorter-lived yellow stars.

The K-type classification matters in three ways.

**Stability.** Vara produces significantly less high-energy radiation than hotter stars and experiences fewer violent flare events. Mira's atmosphere has never needed to withstand the kind of radiation bombardment that strips habitability from worlds orbiting more energetic stars. Life on Mira evolved in calm light.

**Warmth.** K-type stars are cooler than some others, which places their habitable zones closer in. Mira orbits at 1.00 skela'var — a distance that produces a cool temperate climate. The global average temperature on Mira runs around 100 lath. This is not despite Vara's lower output but because of how Mira's atmosphere and orbital distance work together.

**Longevity.** Vara's total stable lifespan is measured in tens of thousands of tael'kora. It is already 4 tael'kora old and will remain on the main sequence for at least 6 tael'kora more — likely closer to 9. Our civilisation has existed for approximately 134,000 thara'keth, which is a fraction of a tael'kora. In Vara's lifespan, we are a very recent development.

## Name and Cultural Meaning

*Vara* derives from the root **var/vara** — star, light. It is the simplest possible name: the star is light, and so it is called light. This naming predates formal language, predates writing, predates our understanding of what a star actually was. The name was not chosen. It simply accumulated.

The informal name *Skela'el'vara* — "our home's star" — is used in conversational and poetic contexts. It is the name used when we mean the warm thing in the sky above Mira rather than the measured astronomical object in the archive record.

In Furling mythology, Vara is not a god or a person. It is more intimate than that — a constant presence, the thing that makes the ground warm, the thing that makes the crops grow, the thing that is simply always there. Furling poetry about Vara tends to be quiet rather than grand. It does not require praise. It simply requires noticing.

The orange-gold light of a K-type star falls differently than a whiter star's light. It is warmer in tone, more amber than white. Furling artists returned to it endlessly — the quality of late-day light on Mira's crystal formations, the way Vara's colour shifts as it sets through the atmosphere, the orange cast on snow.

## The Reference Standard Decision

When Varathal developed the stellar classification system in Era 14, the choice to make Vara the 1.0 reference standard was not scientifically inevitable. It was a philosophical choice.

Other reference candidates existed — a theoretical average star, or a star with round-number physical properties. Varathal rejected both. The purpose of a measurement standard, they argued, was not abstraction but grounding. Every Furling who would ever use the system knew Vara. Every student who learned stellar physics had grown up under it. Making Vara the reference meant that every stellar measurement in the archive carried an implicit comparison: how does this star differ from the one above our heads?

This is consistent with how we anchored all other units — mass to Mira's own mass, distance to Mira's orbital path around Vara. Measurement begins where you stand.

The convention has held for over 118,000 thara'keth without revision.
